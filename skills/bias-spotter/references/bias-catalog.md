# Bias catalog

Lenses for reading a text. Each entry gives: what the bias is, what it looks like on the page, and what commonly gets mistaken for it. The "not this" line matters as much as the signals.

## Contents

1. Evidence and belief: confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, cherry-picking, availability heuristic, anecdotal reasoning, base rate neglect, survivorship bias
2. Judging causes and people: fundamental attribution error, self-serving bias, in-group/out-group bias, halo effect, just-world belief
3. Time and outcomes: hindsight bias, outcome bias, planning fallacy, optimism bias, recency bias, status quo bias
4. Money, effort and commitment: sunk cost, loss aversion, endowment effect, escalation of commitment
5. Confidence and knowledge: overconfidence, Dunning-Kruger pattern, illusion of control, curse of knowledge, false consensus
6. Numbers and patterns: anchoring, framing effect, clustering illusion, gambler's fallacy, correlation read as causation
7. Social: bandwagon effect, authority bias, groupthink
8. Devices aimed at the reader: scarcity, social proof, anchoring price, loss framing, authority cues, identifiable victim

## 1. Evidence and belief

**Confirmation bias**: seeking or weighting evidence that supports what one already believes.
- Signals: every cited source agrees; disconfirming data is absent or waved off in a clause ("critics say X, but obviously..."); ambiguous evidence is read as support; different standards of proof for the two sides.
- Not this: a persuasive piece that argues one side openly, or a text where the evidence really is lopsided.

**Motivated reasoning**: the conclusion comes first and the argument is built to reach it.
- Signals: the author has a visible stake; arguments shift when one is rebutted but the conclusion never moves; "there must be a way to..."
- Not this: having a stake. Interested parties can reason well.

**Cherry-picking**: selecting the favorable slice of data.
- Signals: oddly specific time windows ("since March 2020"), a chosen subgroup, best-case metric switched between paragraphs, a single study standing in for a literature.
- Not this: any use of an example. Look for signs the selection was convenient.

**Availability heuristic**: judging frequency or risk by how easily examples come to mind.
- Signals: vivid, recent or personal cases used to estimate how common something is; "you hear about this all the time"; risk talk driven by news events.
- Not this: an anecdote used to illustrate a claim that is backed elsewhere.

**Anecdotal reasoning**: a personal story treated as general proof.
- Signals: "my uncle smoked and lived to 95"; "it worked for me, so..."
- Not this: an anecdote offered as a counterexample to a universal claim (one case does refute "always").

**Base rate neglect**: ignoring how common something is in general in favor of case specifics.
- Signals: confidence in an individual prediction with no reference to how often such things succeed; test results interpreted without prevalence; "this one is different".
- Not this: a text that states the base rate and argues why the case departs from it.

**Survivorship bias**: drawing lessons only from the cases that made it.
- Signals: "successful founders all dropped out"; advice derived from winners; old things described as better built; no mention of those who did the same and failed.
- Not this: studying successes when failures are also examined.

## 2. Judging causes and people

**Fundamental attribution error**: explaining others' behavior by character and overlooking circumstances.
- Signals: "they're lazy / careless / dishonest" with no look at constraints, incentives or information they had.
- Not this: a character judgment supported by a pattern across situations.

**Self-serving bias**: successes are down to me, failures are down to circumstances.
- Signals: asymmetric explanations within the same text ("we executed well" vs. "the market turned"); credit taken in the active voice, blame given in the passive.
- Not this: an account where external causes are documented and internal ones are also owned.

**In-group / out-group bias**: more charity for one's own side.
- Signals: "we" make mistakes, "they" reveal who they are; the out-group described as uniform ("they all..."); the worst example of the other side set against the best of one's own.
- Not this: criticizing a group for a position it actually holds.

**Halo effect** (and its reverse, the horn effect): one good or bad trait colors everything.
- Signals: expertise in one field treated as authority in another; an attractive, successful or likable person assumed competent or honest; one flaw used to dismiss all of someone's work.

**Just-world belief**: assuming people get what they deserve.
- Signals: victims assumed to have done something to invite it; success read as proof of virtue.

## 3. Time and outcomes

**Hindsight bias**: after the fact, the outcome looks like it was predictable.
- Signals: "it was obvious", "anyone could have seen", "the signs were all there"; no account of what was known at the time.
- Not this: a documented prior warning that was actually ignored.

**Outcome bias**: judging a decision by how it turned out, not by how good it was given what was known.
- Signals: "it worked, so it was the right call"; a failed bet called stupid with no look at the odds.

**Planning fallacy**: underestimating time, cost and risk for one's own project.
- Signals: schedules with no buffer; "should only take two weeks"; past overruns explained away as one-offs; no comparison with similar past projects.
- Not this: an estimate that cites reference cases or includes contingency.

**Optimism bias**: believing bad outcomes are less likely for oneself.
- Signals: risks listed and then dismissed without argument; "that won't happen to us".

**Recency bias**: overweighting the latest data.
- Signals: a trend declared from the last one or two data points; long history ignored.

**Status quo bias**: preferring the current state because it is current.
- Signals: change must prove itself, the existing way need not; "we've always done it this way"; risks of acting listed, risks of not acting omitted.
- Not this: a real argument that switching costs outweigh gains.

## 4. Money, effort and commitment

**Sunk cost fallacy**: continuing because of what has already been spent.
- Signals: "we've already invested..."; "we can't stop now"; "it would all be wasted"; past spend used as a reason about the future.
- Not this: past investment cited because it created a real asset that lowers the future cost.

**Escalation of commitment**: doubling down after setbacks, often to justify the earlier choice.
- Signals: each failure answered with more resources; targets quietly moved; the person who chose the path is the one evaluating it.

**Loss aversion**: losses weigh more than equal gains.
- Signals: a small possible loss treated as decisive against a larger likely gain; the option framed only by what could be lost.

**Endowment effect**: valuing something more because it is mine.
- Signals: own idea, product or possession priced or rated well above comparable ones with no stated reason.

## 5. Confidence and knowledge

**Overconfidence**: more certainty than the evidence supports.
- Signals: "definitely", "guaranteed", "no chance", "there is no doubt"; point predictions with no range; no mention of what could go wrong; complex questions given simple, certain answers.
- Not this: confident language about well-established facts, or a house style that is simply direct.

**Dunning-Kruger pattern**: confidence in a domain the author shows little grasp of.
- Signals: dismissing a whole field ("economists just don't get it") from outside it; "it's simple, really" about hard problems. Rate cautiously: this requires judging competence, so only report it when the text itself shows the gap.

**Illusion of control**: overestimating one's influence over chance outcomes.
- Signals: skill credited for results in domains dominated by luck; rituals or systems for random events.

**Curse of knowledge**: assuming the audience knows what the author knows.
- Signals: unexplained jargon in text for newcomers; "obviously", "as everyone knows" before non-obvious claims.

**False consensus**: assuming most people share one's views.
- Signals: "everyone agrees", "nobody actually wants", "any reasonable person" with no data.

## 6. Numbers and patterns

**Anchoring**: an initial number pulls later judgment toward it.
- Signals: an estimate that stays near an arbitrary starting figure (last year's budget, the first offer, the asking price); "compared with the original quote, this is cheap".

**Framing effect**: the same facts feel different by presentation.
- Signals: "90% survive" vs. "10% die" chosen to suit the argument; relative risk without absolute risk ("doubles the risk" from 1 to 2 in a million); percentages with no base.
- Usually a reader-aimed device (see section 8) unless the author seems misled by their own framing.

**Clustering illusion / seeing patterns in noise**
- Signals: conclusions from small samples; streaks treated as meaningful; "three in a row can't be a coincidence".

**Gambler's fallacy**: believing independent events are "due".
- Signals: "we're due for a win"; "it can't happen again so soon".

**Correlation read as causation**: strictly a reasoning error, often driven by confirmation bias.
- Signals: "since we launched X, Y went up" with no look at other causes, timing or reverse causation.

## 7. Social

**Bandwagon effect**: believing or doing something because many others do.
- Signals: popularity offered as the reason ("everyone is moving to...").

**Authority bias**: over-trusting a claim because of who said it.
- Signals: a name or title substituted for an argument; authority cited outside their field.
- Not this: citing a relevant expert as one piece of evidence.

**Groupthink**: a group suppresses dissent to keep harmony.
- Signals (in meeting notes or team memos): "we all agreed quickly"; no recorded objections on a hard call; dissenters described as not being team players.

## 8. Devices aimed at the reader

Report these in the separate "Persuasion devices" section. The author is using the reader's biases, not showing their own.

- **Scarcity / urgency**: "only 3 left", "offer ends tonight", countdowns.
- **Social proof**: "join 50,000 others", testimonials, "best-selling".
- **Price anchoring**: a struck-through high price beside the real one; a decoy tier that makes another look good.
- **Loss framing**: "don't miss out", "you're losing $X every day".
- **Authority cues**: "doctors recommend", lab coats, credentials without the evidence.
- **Identifiable victim**: one moving story used where statistics would tell a different story.
- **Foot-in-the-door / commitment**: a tiny first ask designed to lead to a bigger one.
- **Fear appeals**: threat vividly described, probability not mentioned.

Common and legitimate marketing uses these. Describe what the device does and let the reader decide; reserve stronger language for cases where the claim is also misleading (fake scarcity, invented numbers), and only if the text itself gives reason to think so.

## Things that are not cognitive biases

- **Logical fallacies** (straw man, ad hominem, false dilemma, slippery slope, circular reasoning). These are faults in argument structure. Mention one only if it matters, and call it a fallacy.
- **Opinions and values.** "Taxes should be lower" is a position, not a bias.
- **Factual errors.** Being wrong is not being biased.
- **Advocacy.** A lawyer's brief or campaign speech is one-sided on purpose.
- **Bias in the everyday sense** (prejudice, slant). If the user means this, say what you see, but do not dress it in cognitive-bias terminology unless it fits.
